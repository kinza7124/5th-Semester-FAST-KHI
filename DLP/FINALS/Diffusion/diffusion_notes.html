Section 01

What is a Diffusion Model?

A diffusion model is a type of generative AI model — meaning it can create new data (like images) from scratch. Think of models like Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 2, and Midjourney — these all use diffusion under the hood.

Imagine you have a beautiful photo. You slowly sprinkle sand over it, step by step, until it's completely buried and you can only see random sand. A diffusion model learns to reverse this process — given a pile of sand, it learns to recover the original photo.

There are two key phases:

Phase 1

🌫️ Forward Process (Noising)

Gradually destroy a real image by adding noise, step by step, until it becomes pure random static. This is fixed — no learning needed.

Phase 2

✨ Reverse Process (Denoising)

Train a neural network to go backwards — starting from noise, progressively clean it up to create a realistic image. This is what we learn.

Diffusion models = learn to reverse a noise-adding process. At inference time, start from pure noise and run the learned reverse to get a new image.
Section 02

Gaussian Noise — The Building Block

Before we talk about noising images, we need to understand what noise we're adding.

What is a Gaussian / Normal Distribution?

A Gaussian distribution (also called normal distribution or bell curve) is a probability distribution parameterized by:

  • μ (mu) = mean (center of the bell)
  • σ² (sigma squared) = variance (how spread out)
𝒩(x ; μ, σ²) = (1 / √(2πσ²)) · exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))
Definition of Gaussian PDF
  • x — the value we're evaluating
  • μ — mean: where the peak is. Usually μ = 0 for noise.
  • σ² — variance: how wide/narrow the bell is. Larger = more spread out.
  • Writing 𝒩(0, I) means mean=0, variance=identity (standard normal)
Figure 1 — Gaussian bell curve. Most values cluster near μ=0, fewer values far away.
When we say "sample from 𝒩(0, I)", we're rolling a dice whose outcomes are mostly near 0, rarely far from 0. Each pixel of noise is one such dice roll.

Mathematical Definition — One Step

Each forward step is defined as:

q(xₜ | xₜ₋₁) = 𝒩(xₜ ; √(1 − βₜ) · xₜ₋₁ , βₜ · I)
Forward process — single step
  • xₜ — image at timestep t
  • xₜ₋₁ — image at previous timestep
  • βₜ — noise schedule, a small number between 0.0001 and 0.02 typically
  • √(1 − βₜ) — scales down the original signal (makes it fade)
  • βₜ · I — variance of noise added
Think of fading a photo on a copier. Each copy fades a little (the √(1−β) term), and introduces a tiny amount of static (the β term). After 1000 copies, you get pure static.

Why √(1−βₜ) as the multiplier?

When you scale a Gaussian by a constant c, its variance scales by c². By choosing c = √(1−β), the signal's variance contribution becomes (1−β), and adding noise of variance β gives total variance approaching 1 — keeping everything normalized! This is crucial for the math to stay clean.

The Noise Schedule βₜ

The noise schedule controls how fast we add noise. Two common choices:

LinearConstant rate increase; simple but crudeβ linearly from 0.0001 → 0.02
CosineSlow start, slow end; better quality (Ho et al.)βₜ = 1 − cos²((t/T + ε)π/2)

Section 04

The Reparameterization Trick

Here's where the most beautiful and important math comes in. Instead of applying 1000 noising steps one by one, there's a shortcut formula that lets us jump straight from x₀ to any timestep xₜ in one shot!

Step 1 — Define αₜ (alpha)

αₜ = 1 − βₜ
Alpha at timestep t

Step 2 — Define ᾱₜ (alpha-bar) — the cumulative product

ᾱₜ = α₁ · α₂ · α₃ · … · αₜ = ∏ᵢ₌₁ᵗ αᵢ
Cumulative product of alphas
  • ᾱₜ starts near 1 (t=0, no noise) and decays toward 0 (t=T, pure noise)
  • It captures the cumulative "how much original signal is left"

Step 3 — The One-Shot Noising Formula (THE KEY EQUATION)

q(xₜ | x₀) = 𝒩(xₜ ; √ᾱₜ · x₀ , (1 − ᾱₜ) · I)
Forward process — any timestep directly
  • √ᾱₜ · x₀ — the original image scaled down; as t grows, ᾱₜ → 0, signal disappears
  • (1 − ᾱₜ) · I — the noise component grows as signal shrinks

In practice we write this as:

xₜ = √ᾱₜ · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱₜ) · ε
Sampling xₜ from x₀ in one step
  • ε ~ 𝒩(0, I) — standard Gaussian noise, sampled fresh
  • This is the reparameterization trick: instead of sampling from a complex distribution, sample ε from simple 𝒩(0,I) and transform it

Where did this come from? (Derivation Sketch)

We apply one step twice and use the fact that a linear combination of Gaussians is Gaussian:

x₁ = √α₁ · x₀ + √(1−α₁) · ε₁ x₂ = √α₂ · x₁ + √(1−α₂) · ε₂ = √α₂ · (√α₁ · x₀ + √(1−α₁) · ε₁) + √(1−α₂) · ε₂ = √(α₁α₂) · x₀ + [√(α₂(1−α₁)) · ε₁ + √(1−α₂) · ε₂] = √ᾱ₂ · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱ₂) · ε ✅
The two noise terms merge into one because Var(aε₁ + bε₂) = a² + b²
The variances of two independent Gaussians add: Var(aε₁ + bε₂) = a²·Var(ε₁) + b²·Var(ε₂). With unit Gaussians: a² + b². Here: α₂(1−α₁) + (1−α₂) = 1 − α₁α₂ = 1 − ᾱ₂. This is why ᾱₜ = ∏αᵢ works so perfectly!
Figure 4 — ᾱₜ decays from 1 (full signal) to ≈0 (pure noise) as t → T.

Why can't we just invert the forward process directly?

The true reverse q(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ) involves Bayes' theorem and requires knowing all possible original images to compute — which is intractable! We use a neural network to approximate it instead.

The True Reverse (with Bayes)

q(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ, x₀) = 𝒩(xₜ₋₁ ; μ̃ₜ(xₜ, x₀) , β̃ₜ · I)
Posterior (tractable when conditioned on x₀)
  • This IS computable IF we know x₀. But at inference time we don't have x₀!
  • Solution: predict x₀ (or equivalently, predict ε) with a neural network

Where the posterior mean μ̃ₜ is:

μ̃ₜ = (√ᾱₜ₋₁ · βₜ · x₀ + √αₜ · (1 − ᾱₜ₋₁) · xₜ) / (1 − ᾱₜ)
Posterior mean — derived via Bayes' theorem on Gaussians
  • It's a weighted average between "what x₀ says" and "what xₜ says"
  • As t decreases, x₀'s contribution grows (we're close to the original)

The Learned Reverse: p_θ

Our neural network parameterizes the reverse as:

p_θ(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ) = 𝒩(xₜ₋₁ ; μ_θ(xₜ, t) , Σ_θ(xₜ, t))
Learned reverse distribution
  • θ — neural network parameters (weights)
  • μ_θ — network predicts the mean of where to step to
  • Σ_θ — often fixed to βₜ·I to simplify (DDPM paper)
Section 06

The Neural Network's Job (ε-Prediction)

The key insight from the DDPM paper (Ho et al., 2020): instead of predicting the mean μ_θ directly, it's better to train the network to predict the noise ε that was added.

Imagine a blurred photo. Instead of predicting "what the clean photo looks like", you predict "what was smeared/blurred" — the noise. Subtract the predicted blur → get the clean photo. It's the same result but this objective is easier to train!

The Substitution

Recall: xₜ = √ᾱₜ · x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ) · ε. Solve for x₀:

x₀ = (xₜ − √(1 − ᾱₜ) · ε) / √ᾱₜ
Express x₀ in terms of ε and xₜ

Substitute into μ̃ₜ, and the posterior mean becomes:

μ_θ(xₜ, t) = (1/√αₜ) · (xₜ − (βₜ / √(1 − ᾱₜ)) · ε_θ(xₜ, t))
Predicted mean parameterized via noise prediction
  • ε_θ(xₜ, t) — the neural network, takes noisy image + timestep, outputs predicted noise
  • The formula says: take xₜ and subtract the predicted noise (scaled appropriately)
Figure 6 — U-Net architecture: encoder compresses, decoder expands, skip connections preserve detail. Timestep t is injected as an embedding.

Why U-Net?

The network takes a noisy image xₜ and a scalar timestep t, and outputs a noise map of the same spatial size. U-Net's encoder-decoder with skip connections is perfect for this because it can capture both local details (skip connections) and global context (bottleneck).

The timestep embedding tells the network "how noisy is this image right now?" This is critical — removing noise at t=900 (very noisy) is very different from t=50 (almost clean). The network needs to know which regime it's in.
Section 07

The Loss Function — What We Optimize

Step 1 — The Full Objective (ELBO)

Diffusion models are trained by maximizing the log-likelihood of data. Using the Evidence Lower BOund (ELBO) from variational inference:

L = 𝔼[− log p_θ(x₀)] ≤ 𝔼[Σₜ KL(q(xₜ₋₁|xₜ,x₀) ‖ p_θ(xₜ₋₁|xₜ))]
ELBO — derived via Jensen's inequality on log p(x)
  • This is a sum of KL divergences: how different is our learned reverse from the true posterior?
  • Minimizing this = maximizing the data likelihood

Step 2 — KL of Gaussians simplifies to MSE

The KL divergence between two Gaussians with the same variance simplifies to a squared distance of means:

KL(𝒩(μ₁, σ²) ‖ 𝒩(μ₂, σ²)) = (1/(2σ²)) · ‖μ₁ − μ₂‖²
KL divergence between same-variance Gaussians

Step 3 — The Simple DDPM Loss

After substituting the ε-parameterization, all the scaling constants simplify and we get the elegant final loss:

L_simple = 𝔼[t, x₀, ε] [ ‖ε − ε_θ(√ᾱₜ·x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ)·ε , t)‖² ]
⭐ The DDPM training objective — just MSE on noise prediction!
  • Sample a real image x₀
  • Sample a random timestep t
  • Sample noise ε ~ 𝒩(0,I)
  • Build noisy image: xₜ = √ᾱₜ·x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ)·ε
  • Ask network to predict ε; penalize the difference (MSE)
  • That's it! Deceptively simple.
The DDPM paper showed that dropping the weighting terms from the full ELBO and using this simple MSE loss leads to better image quality in practice. Sometimes the simplified objective trains better than the "correct" one — empirically more useful signal.

Section 08

Sampling — Generating New Images

At inference time, no real image is given. We start from pure noise and iteratively denoise:

Step 1

Sample initial noise

xT ~ 𝒩(0, I) — pure random Gaussian noise the size of an image.

Step 2

For t = T down to 1:

Predict noise: ε̂ = ε_θ(xₜ, t). Compute mean. Sample z ~ 𝒩(0,I) (add stochasticity). Step backwards.

Step 3

Return x₀

After T steps, x₀ is the generated image!

The DDPM Sampling Formula

xₜ₋₁ = (1/√αₜ) · (xₜ − (βₜ/√(1−ᾱₜ)) · ε_θ(xₜ,t)) + σₜ · z
DDPM reverse sampling step
  • 1/√αₜ — undo the signal scaling
  • (βₜ/√(1−ᾱₜ)) · ε_θ — subtract the predicted noise
  • σₜ · z — add a tiny bit of fresh noise (σₜ = √βₜ or √β̃ₜ); this is what makes it stochastic/diverse
  • At the last step (t=1), set z=0 — don't add noise to the final image!
Why add noise σₜ·z back in? Without it, every run would produce the exact same image from the same noise seed. The stochasticity matches the true diffusion posterior and allows diversity in generated outputs.
Section 09

DDIM — Faster Sampling

DDPM requires T=1000 steps at inference. That's slow. DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models, Song et al. 2021) fixes this by making the reverse process deterministic.

DDPM: each step is like a random walk backwards (stochastic). DDIM: each step is a straight calculated jump (deterministic). Because it's deterministic and doesn't waste steps on randomness, DDIM can skip steps and still get great results.

The DDIM Update Rule

xₜ₋₁ = √ᾱₜ₋₁ · x̂₀(xₜ) + √(1−ᾱₜ₋₁) · ε_θ(xₜ, t)
DDIM deterministic sampling step
  • x̂₀(xₜ) = (xₜ − √(1−ᾱₜ)·ε_θ) / √ᾱₜ — estimate x₀ from xₜ
  • No random z term — fully deterministic
  • Can be applied with stride (only 50 or 100 steps instead of 1000)

Section 10

Conditional Diffusion (Text → Image)

How does "a photo of an astronaut on horseback" guide the model? Two main techniques:

1. Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) — The Modern Standard

Train the U-Net to accept a text conditioning signal c, but randomly drop c during training (replace with null ∅) so the network learns both conditioned and unconditioned behavior.

ε̂_guided = ε_θ(xₜ, ∅) + w · (ε_θ(xₜ, c) − ε_θ(xₜ, ∅))
CFG — interpolate between conditioned and unconditioned predictions
  • c — text embedding (from CLIP, T5, etc.)
  • ∅ — null / empty prompt
  • w — guidance scale (typically 7–12); higher = more faithful to prompt, less diverse
  • The formula says: go towards c and away from ∅ by factor w
Think of w as "how loudly you shout the prompt". w=1 is a whisper (image barely follows). w=10 is a shout (image slavishly follows but may look unnatural). w=7 is the sweet spot.

2. Cross-Attention in U-Net

Text is encoded as token embeddings and injected into the U-Net via cross-attention layers:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d) · V
Cross-attention between image features (Q) and text tokens (K, V)
  • Q — queries from image features (what the image "asks for")
  • K, V — keys and values from text embeddings (what text "offers")
  • Each pixel attends to relevant text tokens → semantic alignment!

Latent Diffusion (Stable Diffusion)

Running diffusion in full pixel space (512×512×3) is expensive. Stable Diffusion operates in a compressed latent space (64×64×4) via a VAE encoder/decoder:

Section 11

⭐ Famous Interview Questions

Click each question to reveal the answer.

What is the core idea behind diffusion models? ▾
Diffusion models define a forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise to data over T steps until it becomes pure noise, then train a neural network to learn the reverse process — iteratively denoising to generate new data. Generation works by sampling random noise and running the learned reverse chain.
What does the neural network predict in DDPM, and why? ▾
The network predicts the noise ε that was added (not x₀ directly). The authors found empirically that predicting ε leads to better image quality than predicting x₀ or the reverse mean μ directly. Conceptually: knowing what was added is equivalent to knowing the clean image (they're linked by xₜ = √ᾱₜ·x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ)·ε), but the noise prediction objective has more stable gradients.
What is the role of ᾱₜ (alpha-bar)? ▾
ᾱₜ = ∏αᵢ (cumulative product of 1−βᵢ) controls how much of the original signal remains at timestep t. At t=0, ᾱₜ≈1 (nearly clean). At t=T, ᾱₜ≈0 (pure noise). Crucially, ᾱₜ allows us to sample xₜ from x₀ in a single step: xₜ = √ᾱₜ·x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ)·ε, bypassing the need to simulate all T intermediate steps during training.
What is Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) and why is w important? ▾
CFG trains a single model to do both conditional and unconditional generation (by randomly dropping conditioning during training). At sampling time: ε̂ = ε_θ(xₜ, ∅) + w·(ε_θ(xₜ, c) − ε_θ(xₜ, ∅)). The scale w (guidance scale) controls the trade-off: w=1 = no guidance boost; w>1 = stronger adherence to prompt at the cost of diversity. Typical range: 7–12 for text-to-image.
How does DDIM differ from DDPM? What's the tradeoff? ▾
DDPM is stochastic (adds noise at each step); DDIM is deterministic (no stochastic term). DDIM's determinism allows skipping timesteps — achieving comparable quality in 20–50 steps vs DDPM's 1000. Tradeoff: DDIM can slightly reduce diversity and sample quality but dramatically improves inference speed. Both use the same trained model — DDIM is just a different sampler.
Why is the forward process fixed (not learned)? ▾
The forward process is a predefined Markov chain with known Gaussian transitions. It's fixed because: (1) it has a closed-form posterior q(xₜ|x₀), making training tractable; (2) it converges to 𝒩(0,I) by design; (3) there's no need to learn it — the complexity is in the reverse. Fixing the forward process means the only learned component is the denoiser.
What is the training objective for DDPM, intuitively? ▾
Minimize the mean squared error between the true noise ε and the predicted noise ε_θ: L = E[‖ε − ε_θ(xₜ, t)‖²]. Intuitively, pick a real image, corrupt it with known noise at a random timestep, then train the network to identify what noise was added. Over millions of training steps, the network learns the statistical structure of images by learning to denoise them.
What is the reparameterization trick and why is it useful? ▾
Instead of sampling from a complex distribution directly, express the sample as a deterministic function of a simple distribution plus a random variable: z = μ + σ·ε, ε~𝒩(0,I). This is useful because: (1) gradients can flow through μ and σ (deterministic part) for backprop; (2) in diffusion, it gives the direct sampling formula xₜ = √ᾱₜ·x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ)·ε, bypassing step-by-step simulation during training.
How does Stable Diffusion differ from standard DDPM? ▾
Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al.) runs diffusion in a compressed latent space (via a pretrained VAE) instead of pixel space. This reduces the spatial dimensions ~8× (e.g. 512→64), making training and inference dramatically cheaper. Text conditioning is added via cross-attention layers in the U-Net using CLIP text embeddings. At generation, the output latent is decoded back to pixels by the VAE decoder.
What is the noise schedule and why does it matter? ▾
The noise schedule {β₁, ..., βT} determines how quickly signal is destroyed. Too fast (aggressive linear) → the network must deal with badly corrupted images early and small details are lost. Too slow → T steps aren't enough to reach pure noise. Improved cosine schedule (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021) keeps ᾱₜ decaying slowly in mid-range timesteps, giving the network more "useful" noisy images to train on and improving sample quality.
Compare diffusion models vs GANs vs VAEs. ▾
GANs: fast sampling (1 forward pass), high sharpness, but training instability, mode collapse, no likelihood.
VAEs: stable training, explicit likelihood, but blurry outputs (Gaussian decoder assumption).
Diffusion: state-of-the-art quality, diversity, and mode coverage; stable training; explicit likelihood via ELBO. Downside: slow sampling (1000 steps), high compute. DDIM mitigates the speed issue.
What is score matching and how does it relate to diffusion? ▾
Score matching learns the score function ∇_x log p(x) — the gradient of log data density. Song et al. showed that predicting noise ε in DDPM is equivalent to estimating the score: ε_θ ≈ −√(1−ᾱₜ)·∇_xₜ log q(xₜ). This connection means diffusion models implicitly do score-based generative modeling, and sampling is like gradient ascent on a time-varying log-density landscape (Langevin dynamics).
